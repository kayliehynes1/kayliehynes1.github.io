MSc Dissertation · 2026

Deep Learning Based Detection of Brain Tumors in MRI Images

BraTS 2021 + 2024MRI · T1 / T1ce / T2 / FLAIRPyTorch3D U-NetComplete

The Question

Before surgery or radiation, radiologists need a precise outline of a brain tumor on MRI, and drawing that outline by hand, slice by slice, can take hours. Deep learning models can do it in seconds. But most of them are trained and tested on scans taken at diagnosis, before any treatment has happened. I wanted to find out how well these models really perform, and whether one trained on pre-treatment scans could still find tumors in patients who had already been treated.

Data

My training data came from BraTS 2021, a widely used collection of pre-treatment glioma scans in which experts have carefully labeled every tumor. To test the model on something new, I used 417 patients from BraTS 2024, whose scans were all taken after treatment. Each patient has four types of MRI scan. The labels split every tumor into three regions, the whole tumor, the tumor core, and the enhancing tumor, which is the part that lights up when a contrast dye is given.

Approach

I worked in stages. First, I built a 2D U-Net, which looks at the brain one flat slice at a time, to set a baseline. Then I built a 3D U-Net that takes in the whole brain volume at once, so it can follow a tumor's shape from one slice to the next. I trained both models on the university's GPU cluster. Next came the real test. I ran the 3D model on the post-treatment scans without any retraining, to see how it would cope with images it had never been prepared for. Along the way, I used Grad-CAM heatmaps to look inside the models and check which parts of each scan they were paying attention to.

Results

To measure accuracy, I used the Dice score, which compares the model's outline with the expert's. A score of 1 means the two match perfectly. A score of 0 means they don't overlap at all.

ModelTest dataMean Dice
2D U-NetBraTS 2021 (pre-treatment)0.648
3D U-NetBraTS 2021 (pre-treatment)0.911
3D U-NetBraTS 2024 (post-treatment)0.471

The jump from 2D to 3D was striking. Mean Dice rose from 0.65 to 0.91, which puts my 3D U-Net in the same range as well-known published models.

The post-treatment scans told a different story. Scores fell to 0.60 for the whole tumor and to around 0.40 for the tumor core and enhancing regions. Much of that drop comes down to surgery. Removing a tumor leaves behind a resection cavity, and because the model had never seen one during training, it had no idea what to make of it.

What I Learned

This project taught me that a strong score on one dataset doesn't guarantee a model will hold up in the real world. Treatment changes how tumors look on MRI, sometimes dramatically. If these tools are going to help patients, they need to be trained and tested across the whole course of care, not only at diagnosis. That gap between a benchmark and the clinic is a big part of why I want to keep working in cancer research.

hTDP-43 3D Synapse Analysis

Counting synapses on motor neurons in 3D light-sheet microscopy of an ALS mouse model

Light-Sheet MicroscopyCellposescikit-imagePythonPipeline Complete

The Question

In ALS, the motor neurons that control the body's muscles slowly stop working and die. One idea is that these neurons lose some of their "brakes," the inhibitory connections that tell them not to fire, and become overexcited. The Allodi lab had measured these connections in flat 2D slices of spinal cord. My job was to build a pipeline that could count them in 3D, across a whole block of tissue, for every individual motor neuron.

Background

Neurons talk to each other at contact points called synapses. Each synapse has a sending side and a receiving side. In this project, two proteins mark the two sides of an inhibitory synapse. GlyT2 sits on the sending side, and Gephyrin sits on the receiving side. Under the microscope, each one shows up as a tiny bright dot, so a working synapse should appear as a GlyT2 dot right next to a Gephyrin dot on the surface of a motor neuron.

The tissue came from hTDP-43 mice. TDP-43 is a protein that clumps up abnormally in nearly all ALS cases, and these mice carry the human version, so they develop ALS-like problems.

Data

The images were taken with a light-sheet microscope, which scans a thick piece of tissue one thin layer at a time. The full stack holds 921 slices spaced 3 µm apart, with three channels per slice, one for neurons, one for GlyT2, and one for Gephyrin. That adds up to thousands of very large image files and hundreds of gigabytes of data, all processed on a single lab workstation.

Approach

The pipeline has three main steps. First, I trained a custom Cellpose model to find motor neurons. I outlined about 82 neurons by hand so the model could learn what they look like in this tissue, then ran it on every slice. Second, I detected the GlyT2 and Gephyrin dots with blob detection. I first tried a dedicated tool called SynQuant, but it didn't hold up well on this data, so I switched to a method I could tune and run in batches. Third, I matched every dot to its nearest neuron and counted how many each neuron received.

Finding and Fixing a Problem

The first results looked very strong. GlyT2 and Gephyrin counts were tightly linked, and the statistics were highly significant. But more than half of all neurons had zero synapses, which didn't make sense. When I reviewed the pipeline before handing it over, I found out why. Neurons were found across the whole image, but synapse dots were only detected inside a smaller cropped region. So about 77% of neurons never had a chance to receive any dots at all.

I reran the statistics using only the neurons inside the checked region. The zeros mostly disappeared, and the strong results went away with them.

All neuronsChecked region only
Neuron cross-sections79,46018,140

What the Results Show

With the fix in place, a typical neuron received about 20 to 40 dots, and overall there was roughly one sending dot for every receiving dot. But neuron by neuron, the two counts don't line up. If these were true synapses, a neuron with many GlyT2 dots should also have many Gephyrin dots, since every synapse has both sides. They also rise and fall in opposite directions through the depth of the tissue. To me, that says the pipeline is counting dots near each neuron, but not yet true paired synapses.

Limitations and Next Steps

These results describe the hTDP-43 tissue only, so they can't say anything about ALS until the same pipeline runs on healthy control mice. Each neuron is also counted once per slice rather than once per cell, which makes the statistics look stronger than they should. I've documented the next steps in the project's README so another student can pick it up. They include linking each neuron across slices into a single 3D cell, counting only dots touching a neuron's edge, checking the dot detection against hand counts, and running the control data.

What I Learned

This was my first time working with biological images, and my first time handling data this large. It taught me that results can look convincing for the wrong reasons. The numbers that seemed strongest turned out to come from a mismatch between two steps of my own pipeline, and I only caught it because I went back to check why so many neurons had zero synapses. Reporting that honestly, and handing over a clear list of what still needs fixing, felt more useful than a clean-looking result I couldn't trust.

What a Tumor's Shape Says About Its Genes

Linking glioma shape on MRI to genetic subtypes in 110 patients

TCGA Lower-Grade GliomaU-NetRadiogenomicsPyTorchComplete

The Question

Can the shape of a brain tumor on an MRI scan tell us something about its genetics? Today, the only way to learn a tumor's genetic makeup is to take a piece of it during surgery and send it to a lab. If the shape a tumor takes on a routine scan reflects what is happening inside its genes, an MRI could one day offer early clues before anyone picks up a scalpel. This idea is called radiogenomics, and this project puts it to the test on a public set of 110 patients.

Background

Gliomas are tumors that start in glial cells, the cells that support and protect the brain's neurons. Lower-grade gliomas grow more slowly than the glioblastomas from my dissertation, but they often become more aggressive over time, which is why doctors follow them so closely.

The Cancer Genome Atlas, a large US research project, measured several kinds of activity inside each of these tumors. Some measurements look at which genes are switched on, others at chemical tags that switch genes off, and others at chunks of DNA that have gone missing or been copied. For each kind of measurement, a computer sorted the patients into groups, called clusters, whose tumors looked alike. Tumors in different clusters can behave very differently. They may also grow differently. Some push outward as a smooth, compact mass, while others creep into the surrounding brain with uneven, finger-like edges. If those growth patterns follow the clusters, the difference should show up in the tumor's shape.

Data

I used a public dataset released alongside a 2019 study by Buda, Saha, and Mazurowski. It holds 3,929 MRI slices from 110 patients, each with three scan types (pre-contrast, FLAIR, and post-contrast), an expert's outline of the tumor on every slice, and each patient's genetic clusters from The Cancer Genome Atlas.

Approach

The project had four main steps. First, I trained a U-Net, the same kind of model as in my dissertation, to outline each tumor automatically. To make the test fair, I split the patients into five groups and trained five separate models, each one tested only on patients it had never seen. Every patient ended up with an outline drawn by a model that had no prior look at their brain.

Second, I measured each tumor's shape in five ways. Third, I tested whether those shapes differed between genetic clusters. Finally, I ran every test twice, once using the expert's outlines and once using the model's, to see whether an automatic pipeline would reach the same conclusions as a person drawing by hand.

Measuring Shape

Each measure boils a tumor outline down to a single number. Before using them on real tumors, I checked every one on simple test shapes like a circle, an oval, and a star, to make sure each number captured what it was supposed to.

MeasureWhat it describes
SizeTotal tumor area across all slices
RoundnessHow close the outline is to a perfect circle
SolidityHow filled-in the shape is, compared with a rubber band stretched around it
ElongationHow stretched out the shape is
Edge irregularityHow bumpy the edge is, once the overall shape is set aside

Finding and Fixing Failures

The first version of the model did well on most patients but missed the tumor completely in 10 of them. Rather than move on, I went back and compared those 10 patients with everyone else. Two causes stood out. Their scans were much darker overall, so the tumors never looked bright enough for the model to notice. Several were also missing one or two scan types, which the dataset fills in with copies of the FLAIR scan, an image the model had rarely seen during training.

The second version fixed both. It evens out the brightness of each patient's scans before training, and it randomly swaps scan types for copies of the FLAIR during training so the model learns to cope. Nine of the 10 failures came back, most of them with a Dice score above 0.5. The fixes did not lift the model across the board, though. Across the other 100 patients, about as many got slightly worse as got better, which is a useful reminder that every training run lands a little differently.

Results

Tumor shape does track genetics in this dataset. Of the 35 combinations of shape measure and genetic cluster, 12 showed a significant difference between groups, even after a correction for running so many tests at once. The clearest pattern involved RNASeqCluster, the grouping based on which genes are switched on. Tumors in group 1 formed the most compact masses with the smoothest edges. Tumors in group 2 were the least solid, with the most dents and inlets.

Size also differed between groups. One protein-based group had tumors about two and a half times larger than the others. These size results are new, though, since the original study did not test them, so I treat them as leads rather than firm findings.

Expert vs. Model Outlines

When I reran every test using the model's outlines instead of the expert's, 11 of the 12 findings held up. That matters because it means the whole pipeline, from raw scan to genetic clue, can run without a person drawing anything by hand. The model does have one consistent habit, though. It draws smoother outlines than the expert, rounding off the fine, wiggly details at the edge of a tumor. Since edge detail is part of what is being linked to genetics, that habit is worth keeping in mind.

Comparison With the Original Study

The 2019 study behind this dataset found two shape features linked to RNASeqCluster. One captured how compact a tumor is, and the other captured how bumpy its edge is. Working independently, with my own model, my own shape code, and a different statistical test, I found the same two links, and they were my two strongest results. Both studies also singled out RNASeq group 2 as having the most irregular tumors.

Limitations

These results show a link between shape and genetics, not that one causes the other. The genetic clusters also overlap with each other, so the 12 significant results are better read as a few consistent patterns than as 12 separate discoveries. All 110 patients come from a single collection, and any finding would need to hold up in a new group of patients before it could mean anything in a clinic. Finally, my model still falls a little short of the original study's segmentation accuracy.

What I Learned

This project taught me as much about checking my own work as about building it. The first model's average score looked reasonable, and it would have been easy to move on. Looking closely at the failures is what revealed the dark scans and missing scan types. It also showed me what a careful comparison looks like, from testing shape measures on simple shapes before trusting them, to reporting the patients who got worse alongside the ones who got better. Above all, it was my first real step from medical images into genetics, and seeing the two line up in my own results is exactly why I want to keep working in cancer research.

Reading Notes

Papers I've Read

Summaries of the papers that shape how I think about my work.

Oct 2026

Association of Genomic Subtypes of Lower-Grade Gliomas With Shape Features Automatically Extracted by a Deep Learning Algorithm

Buda M, Saha A, Mazurowski MA. Computers in Biology and Medicine, 2019.

The question. Lower-grade gliomas fall into genetic subtypes that behave very differently, but finding out which subtype a patient has requires a tissue sample. The authors asked whether the shape of the tumor on a routine MRI could hint at its subtype, and whether a computer could measure that shape on its own, without an expert drawing the outline by hand.

What they did. They gathered MRI scans from 110 patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas and trained a deep learning model, a U-Net, to outline each tumor on the FLAIR scan. The model's outlines matched an expert's closely, with a mean Dice score of 82% and a median of 85%. From those automatic outlines they measured three shape features. One captured how much of a snug, oval wrapper the tumor filled in 3D (the bounding ellipsoid volume ratio). The other two captured how uneven the distance from the tumor's center to its edge was (angular standard deviation) and how bumpy the edge was once the overall shape was smoothed away (margin fluctuation). They then tested ten pairs of shape features and genetic subtypes chosen in advance, using a strict cutoff to guard against chance findings.

What they found. Two links passed the strict cutoff, and both involved the subtype based on gene activity, RNASeqCluster. Less compact tumors were tied to particular gene-activity groups, and so were tumors with bumpier edges. One group, R2, stood out for its irregular shapes, and the compactness measure alone could pick out R2 tumors fairly well. The authors described these links as moderate. They were real and measurable, but not strong enough to replace a tissue test.

Why it matters. The study showed that a fully automatic pipeline, from raw scan to shape measurement, could pick up genetic signals that until then had required a lab. It also released its data publicly, which made it possible for others to build on the work.

My takeaway. This paper was the starting point for my What a Tumor's Shape Says About Its Genes project. I used its dataset, built my own model and shape measures, and found the same two links to RNASeqCluster, including the same irregular group. Reproducing a published result from scratch showed me how much care goes into each step, and how much a clear, open dataset can do for other researchers.
